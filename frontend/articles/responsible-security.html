FIELD NOTE / 03 / UNITED KINGDOM · LONDON

Architecture and transit help verify a candidate without claiming certainty.

Similar facades repeat across a city. The useful question is whether several independent, fixed features line up at the same candidate location.

WhereWas field notes · Illustrative example · 3 min read

  • Facade geometry
  • Street furniture
  • Transit signs when legible

Stone facades and an open paved area give this London reference scene a recognizable character. But a general architectural style is rarely enough to identify a street. Reliable review starts by describing the scene, then checking whether a candidate reproduces its specific arrangement.

Look beyond a London-like appearance.

Record the shape of the roofline, window spacing, entrances and the relationship between buildings and the pavement. Similar buildings can exist on adjoining streets or in another city entirely. A broad resemblance should influence a shortlist without being treated as a verified match.

In other London scenes, a legible station name, bus-stop label or route destination can offer additional context. Do not invent transit clues when none are readable in the uploaded frame. A red bus alone is less specific than a named stop beside a distinctive building.

Test the viewing direction.

A useful comparison checks the order of objects as well as their appearance. Does the entrance sit to the same side of the junction? Is the neighboring facade visible from that angle? Can a fixed lamp, crossing or corner be placed consistently in both scenes? A panorama may need rotation before that comparison is meaningful.

The strongest candidate explains the arrangement of the scene, not only one recognizable object.

Keep an investigation reviewable.

For place verification, record the candidate, the evidence that supports it and anything that disagrees. Reference imagery may differ because of renovation, capture date or camera position. A result should retain uncertainty instead of turning a plausible resemblance into a certain street address.

WhereWas is designed for photos of places. Incidental pedestrians do not automatically prevent analysis. When a person is prominent, the engine limits the result to a broad city-level radius rather than running fine-grained matching. This distinction matters when using location evidence in research or an authorized security workflow.

This is an illustrative field note, not an account of a real investigation or a claim that the pictured scene was independently located by the engine.

Read the evidence, not just the pin.

These field notes describe a review process, not a measured success rate. Nearby street imagery is not proof of a visual match. Candidate coordinates should be interpreted with their confidence, supporting evidence and radius.
