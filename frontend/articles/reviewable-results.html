FIELD NOTE / 02 / INDIA · MUMBAI

A street sign, pavement and traffic pattern provide separate clues.

A place name is a starting point. Surrounding street details help distinguish a plausible Mumbai candidate from a confident but incorrect match.

WhereWas field notes · Illustrative example · 3 min read

  • Street-name text
  • Pavement layout
  • Neighboring features

A street sign can be the strongest clue in a photograph, but it still needs interpretation. The Mumbai reference image pairs a roadside sign with the pavement and surrounding street. Those details offer independent checks rather than three versions of the same guess.

Keep language and location separate.

Marathi and English text can guide a search when it is legible. Devanagari script on its own is not proof of Mumbai: it is used in several languages across a broad region. OCR can also confuse similar characters, abbreviations or weathered lettering. Compare the transcription with the photo before trusting the resulting place name.

A named road can have multiple spellings or share its name with a road elsewhere. Combine it with other visible text and a regional hypothesis instead of selecting the first name match.

Check the candidate against the pavement.

Look at where the sign stands relative to the curb, nearby entrances and intersecting roads. The width and continuity of a footpath, a distinctive corner or a fixed roadside structure may help separate otherwise similar candidates. Traffic direction can be useful context, while individual vehicles are temporary.

A text match proposes the location. The surrounding scene has to support it.

Compare, then keep the uncertainty visible.

  1. Inspect the most specific readable text and retain alternative spellings when needed.
  2. Compare the map's junction arrangement and the position of neighboring features.
  3. Use available reference imagery to test whether the same fixed features appear together.

If the sign fits but the road geometry does not, keep looking. Reference imagery can also be old or face the opposite direction. Missing coverage is a limit on verification, not evidence that a location is wrong.

WhereWas presents candidates with evidence and a radius so the reviewer can make that distinction. This field note illustrates the workflow; it does not report a measured accuracy score or promise an exact address from the example.

Read the evidence, not just the pin.

These field notes describe a review process, not a measured success rate. Nearby street imagery is not proof of a visual match. Candidate coordinates should be interpreted with their confidence, supporting evidence and radius.
