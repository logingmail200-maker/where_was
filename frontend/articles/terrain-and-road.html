FIELD NOTE / 04 / GEOGRAPHY · SWITZERLAND

Terrain, vegetation and road design matter when there is no storefront.

Geography can narrow the search even without readable shop names. It is most useful when tested alongside road infrastructure and other independent clues.

WhereWas field notes · Illustrative example · 3 min read

  • Terrain and vegetation
  • Road infrastructure
  • Signage and markings

The Swiss reference image shows a road, an overpass and roadside vegetation. Unlike a shop-lined street, it offers few business names to search. The useful evidence comes from the relationship between the landscape, built infrastructure and any signs that remain readable.

Describe the environment without overclaiming.

Start with visible facts: the slope of the land, the openness of the surroundings, tree form and ground cover. Vegetation can suggest a climate or season, but many species grow across borders or are planted far from their native range. A small or blurred plant should not become a confident species identification.

The sky is also weak evidence by itself. Weather changes quickly and similar conditions occur in many regions. Use the landscape as a regional constraint, then seek a more specific clue.

Let road design narrow the possibilities.

Compare lane markings, barriers, sign layouts and the way an overpass meets the road. Look for a readable route number or destination before assuming that a road style belongs to one country. Multiple infrastructure clues together can be more useful than a single patch of greenery.

Geography helps narrow the map. Verification determines how tightly the result can be drawn.

Know when the radius should stay wide.

  1. Build regional hypotheses from the environment and road context.
  2. Use specific text or structural details to distinguish candidates.
  3. Compare available ground imagery for the same road shape, junction or fixed landmark.

If several regions fit equally well, a broad shortlist is the honest result. A rural scene with no distinctive reference coverage may remain unresolved. More decimal places in a coordinate do not make that estimate more accurate.

WhereWas keeps location candidates, supporting evidence and map review together. This example explains how geographic clues can guide that review; it does not promise that every mountain, field or roadside plant reveals an exact location.

Read the evidence, not just the pin.

These field notes describe a review process, not a measured success rate. Nearby street imagery is not proof of a visual match. Candidate coordinates should be interpreted with their confidence, supporting evidence and radius.
